# Guru Check

Almost nothing in this market is fraud in the legal sense. The video is real. The income screenshot may be real. Someone once made money the way the page is teaching. Most of the money spent on courses and communities is still wasted, because "is it a scam?" is the wrong question. It points you at the exact proof the funnel was built to pass.

The right question is narrower: **will the mechanism that made this person money still run when you do it yourself, and will they still be there when you need them?**

Three failure modes get bundled together, and that is how checks miss:

1. **Fake guru.** They never did the work, or they did it once, long ago, under conditions that no longer exist.
2. **Real guru, dead community.** The knowledge is real. The thing being sold (access, feedback, momentum, a room) has already gone quiet.
3. **Real guru, living community, bad fit.** The mechanism does not survive contact with your capital, your market, or your language.

This skill hunts red flags and it hunts the opposite. Most hidden gems get thrown out by anti-scam filters, because those filters punish people who do not perform.

## Why the usual check fails

The default check does three things: count members, read testimonials on the sales page, search "name + scam." All three are the proof a funnel is built to survive.

- **Member count.** On Skool, member count feeds the Skool Games rank, which tracks owner MRR. A sudden jump is often a 30-day sales sprint, not a mature room.
- **Testimonials on the site.** The founder picked them. Nobody pins the bad one.
- **Search "name + scam."** You get their SEO, their affiliates, or noise from a rival. You do not get an independent record.

Trustworthy proof is proof they cannot manufacture alone.

## When the target is a creator-support platform

Patreon, Ko-fi, Substack, and their cousins are built so people can fund creative work the person is already doing. They are not "buy my system and you will get my life." The risks change with that.

Ask these two first:

1. **Is this the real creator?** The common failure is a page wearing someone else's name, photo, and bio. The real channel (their site, their YouTube, their main social account) has to link to this page on purpose. A page that claims an identity, with no path back from the real channel, is an automatic skip.
2. **Have they actually delivered?** New pages that take money and then post almost nothing are a different death from a Skool that dies after a real product existed. Read the post history. Did they ship what the tier promised, on a rhythm you can see?

Two more traps for this kind of platform are in [references/signal-catalog.md](references/signal-catalog.md).

## Follow the money, not the trophy

The heaviest question in the whole check: **what share of their income comes from doing the thing they teach, and what share comes from teaching it?**

A practitioner who also teaches is one product. A specialist whose only checkable income is tuition is a different product, however good the lessons are. Advice from someone who pays nothing if the advice is wrong deserves less weight than advice from someone who still has to live with the result. That is the skin-in-the-game test, applied to a sales page.

**A self-published number is a claim, not proof.** Screenshots are free, and they can be fabricated. In trading, the hard standard is a track record a third party can verify, not a demo account. Generalize that: proof is something they cannot stage alone. A public repo. A public portfolio. A customer who confirms the result outside the founder's own channels. Anything they screenshot, write, or edit themselves stays a claim until something else corroborates it.

A related flag: "as seen in Forbes / Entrepreneur / Yahoo Finance." A lot of those logos are paid contributor-network posts, a few hundred dollars for the placement. That proves a marketing budget. It does not prove the skill.

**The same standard binds the person running this check.** A specific number inside a search-engine summary is not verified until you open the original page and see the number there, with a source. That includes numbers that support the verdict you are already leaning toward. Uneven skepticism is how a flattering case gets a soft check and a suspicious case gets a hard one. Before you use a figure, ask: if this case were leaning the other way, would I still trust this number?

## Judge by the students, not the founder

A real practitioner can still be a bad teacher. Stronger than the founder's own record: **a named student, starting from a place like yours, with a result after they joined, found outside the founder's channels.**

Sports coaching has a mature version of this. Lineage. You judge a coach by who their students became, and by whether other coaches send students to them. Same test online. Do former students get recognized by peers in the niche, or does only the founder praise them?

Washed testimonials, two shapes:

- Praise for the **atmosphere** ("great energy," "so supportive") instead of a **result** (a number, a date, a name). That is a review of the vibe.
- Praise from someone who sells a similar product. Check the testimonial author's bio for an affiliate tell.

## Guru fingerprints

Kavanagh and Browne, in Decoding the Gurus, describe a set of secular-guru behaviors. Four of them transfer cleanly to coaches and course creators, because they are rhetorical habits, not subject-matter claims:

- **Galaxy-brain.** Confident claims across unrelated fields at once (ecommerce, mindset, crypto, relationships, biohacking). The width of the confidence runs opposite to the depth of any one claim.
- **Grievance, and the Cassandra pose.** "They don't want you to know this." "I was banned for telling the truth." That builds an excuse for the next failure, and it inoculates the audience against later criticism.
- **Everything you were taught is wrong.** A practitioner disagrees with a specific claim, for a specific reason, and can still say what in the old knowledge is true. A performer cannot name the part that still holds.
- **Pseudo-profound language.** A sentence that sounds deep and dies when you restate it. Test: rewrite their central claim as one concrete action a beginner could take today. If you cannot, there is no content under the sentence.

The manufactured-authority sequence has been described in public by people who bought the products in order to take them apart: free webinar, a long session that waits until the viewer is tired, a cheap front-end anchored to a fake "original price" 6 to 10 times higher, then upsells (mastermind, live event, "advanced"), prices ending in 7, and a scarcity timer that resets on an evergreen page.

One active test on top of that: search a few phrases copied **verbatim** from the sales page. If they match free YouTube or blog posts almost word for word, the paid product is a repackage. Then run the **Wayback Machine** on their domain. A niche that keeps pivoting ("10-year Amazon FBA expert" whose 2023 site sold Instagram growth and whose 2025 site sells AI agents) is a warning. Old authority is a real signal. Authority that appeared last season, aimed at a hot niche, is a warning.

## Two active probes

Most of this skill is passive. These two are active, free inside a free group or a DM, and hard to fake:

**Overclaiming probe.** Ask about a concept, a tool, or a "recent update" that sounds plausible and **does not exist** in that niche. A practitioner says they have not heard of it and asks what it is. A performer says yes, it is in module 4. This leans on the overclaiming technique from personality research: people protecting a high self-image will claim to know things that are not real.

**Calibration probe.** Ask "when does this NOT work?" and "who should NOT buy this?" A practitioner answers fast, with a specific limit, and the answer does not help the sale. A vague "of course I can be wrong sometimes" does not count. That is a verbal habit. You want a named limit.

## Read whether the community is alive

Clinical death has a predictable order: **the founder goes quiet first, then the room follows.** The earliest signal that is hard to fake is **founder-reply recency** (the last time they answered someone else's thread), not founder-post recency (posts can be scheduled, or written by a team). A broadcast from three days ago plus a last real reply from six weeks ago means they have already left, and the marketing has not caught up.

Rough thresholds:

| Signal | Healthy | Dying |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly member churn | under 5% | over 10% |
| Members who post in a month | 20–30% or more | under 20% |
| Event attendance | over 15% | under 10% |
| How long a thread stays alive | days | dead in 24–48h |
| Last founder reply | a few days | over a month |

Skool-specific: Skool Games ranks owners by MRR, capped around $100 per member per month. That rewards a pump-and-dump 30-day sprint, not retention. **A recent member spike plus a price near $97–$100 is a yellow flag on Skool, not a green one.**

Also look at: the ratio of teaching posts to selling posts in the founder's last ~20 posts, whether the **future** event calendar is empty (a full past calendar says nothing about whether they still show up), and whether members create posts that other members answer, or the feed is only the founder and staff.

## Finding the hidden gem

Every anti-fake test above punishes silence. A good practitioner with no content machine will fail them, not because they are bad, but because they are not playing that game.

The novice/expert problem, from Alvin Goldman: an outsider cannot directly grade an expert claim, so they have to use indirect evidence. Goldman lists five sources: the quality of the argument and how they handle rebuttal, **agreement from other experts**, judgment from meta-experts, evidence of interest and bias, and track record. Fame is not on the list.

Signals you can actually check:

- **Peers mention them when nobody paid for the mention.** Search the name and exclude their own channels. You want the mention inside someone else's conversation: "go ask X," a reply in a thread that was not about them, a podcast that was not their interview. This is the strongest signal in the skill, because it is the one you cannot buy.
- **Mentions divided by self-promotion.** A fake guru has a huge denominator (their own posts) and a tiny numerator once you remove affiliates. A hidden gem is mentioned more than they talk about themselves.
- **The reputation predates the hot niche.** Date the earliest credible mention (Wayback, the oldest forum thread, the first conference) and compare it to when the niche got money and attention. That separates a reputation built over time from a reputation staged for a trend.
- **They are still doing the work.** Find current work that is not teaching, with a recent date. Shipping, clients, and the occasional public loss keep the lessons tied to a live feedback loop.
- **They have publicly said they were wrong.** Admitting a specific limit, while staying accurate, raises trust rather than lowering it. Find one post where they said "I was wrong about X." A fake guru rarely does this. The product they sell is being right.
- **Their free material already worked for you.** Before you pay, did the free work produce a result, or was the free work only "why you need a system"?

The same behavior reads two ways, depending on whether the positive signals above are present:

| What you see | Fake-guru reading | Hidden-gem reading |
|---|---|---|
| Few followers, rough video | Not established enough | Does not need to perform, because work arrives by word of mouth |
| Posts rarely | Neglect, the room is dying | Busy doing the job, does not live on content |
| No third-party track record | Unverified, stay suspicious | You have to ask real relationships, not Google |
| Names a limit out loud | Rare for a fake guru | A positive signal, see above |

Two warnings, so this does not become a romance about quiet people:

1. **Silence is not skill.** Low promotion only earns "keep looking." You still need at least one positive signal: an unsolicited peer mention, a reputation older than the trend, or proof they are still doing the work. If you find nothing positive, silence is only silence.
2. **Being good at the work is not the same as being good at teaching.** That is why lineage still matters. Did their students get better, or do peers respect only the founder's own craft?

## The axis most course rubrics drop

Nearby public rubrics score four product axes: depth, a clear refund, the founder's track record, student results. The missing axis is the person: whether they perform a character that the product does not require. Keep it separate. Folding it into one score hides the two dangerous cells:

- **Good content, low honesty.** The information may be real, and the relationship is extractive. The room dies in a few months. Buy the content on its own if it is sold that way. Do not buy the community.
- **Rough production, high honesty.** Often the hidden gem. Ugly screen recordings, no funnel, frequently worth more than the polished alternative.

## Anti-patterns

**Language:**

- "Ten years, condensed into 90 days." A promise to skip the path.
- "You used to be exactly where I was." A sympathy story that looks the same in every niche.
- "Everything is in your control. No excuses." A real teacher can name the factors outside your control.
- "The other courses taught it wrong. You were lied to." Selling by attacking rivals, instead of showing their own result.
- A specific income promise plus "refund if you don't hit it." Treat that as a claim, and read the refund conditions before you treat it as protection.
- Your doubt gets relabeled as "your limiting belief." This is the most reliable tell in the list. The act of checking becomes your defect.
- "X spots left, cart closes at midnight," repeating on an evergreen page.

**Structure:**

- Price ends in 7, anchored to a fake "was" price 6 to 10 times higher.
- A cheap entry ($47–$297) that climbs to a $5,000–$30,000 call.
- A "live" webinar that is a recording, with scripted chat.
- 60 to 90 minutes of presentation before the price appears.
- No refund, or a refund that requires finishing every module and submitting proof of work.
- A community under six months old, already priced as a premium.
- Testimonials that praise the vibe and never a result.
- Press logos from paid contributor networks.
- Revenue language kept blurry on purpose (GMV instead of profit, "six figures" instead of a number).

## How to answer

Open every real check with this table, then the evidence. Do not bury the verdict under the research.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Label** | One of the four labels below |
| **Verdict** | BUY, WAIT, or SKIP, plus one sentence on why |
| **Why** | One or two sentences. The heaviest evidence. |
| **Money at risk if wrong** | low / medium / high, tied to the actual price |
| **Next action** | A specific next step |

Four labels. Do not invent a fifth.

- **FAKE GURU.** An automatic-skip trigger fired. Enough to walk away.
- **NEED MORE EVIDENCE.** WAIT. Not enough either way.
- **REAL, VERIFIED.** Identity and current work check out. They do not have to be quiet or unknown.
- **HIDDEN GEM.** Only when both are true: reputation by word of mouth, and they are not performing fame. A well-known creator can still be a BUY. Do not call every good BUY a hidden gem.

## Alternatives by price tier

Use this for "buy a system or a skill" (Skool, a course site). Do not force it onto "fund this specific creator" (Patreon and the like). A skill has other teachers. A specific person does not have a substitute in that sense.

When you use it, list three levels: **Free** (often enough for a common skill), **Same price**, **Higher** (usually a clearer structure: scheduled Q&A, a fixed calendar). Always say this: **the alternatives have not been run through this skill. They are not safer by default. They are where to look next.**

## Two worked examples

**Case 1. A polished Skool that passes the naive check.**
Naive: 8,000 members, testimonials across the landing page, $97 a month. Closer look: the founder has not replied to a single comment in six weeks (broadcasts only, posted by a team), the community is four months old, and 90% of the founder's public revenue is the course itself rather than the work. Verdict: SKIP, even though every surface number looks fine.

**Case 2. A teacher with few followers and phone video, who fails a traditional red-flag list and passes the hidden-gem test.**
Naive: 800 followers, no glossy sales page, no testimonials on a site because there is no course site. Closer look: three different people in the niche name them when asked who is actually good, and none of the three has an affiliate link. The oldest respectful forum mention predates the niche getting hot by three years. They are still doing the work, with a project updated this month. They wrote, in public, what was wrong with the way they taught last year. Verdict: worth a serious look, even though they fail every surface status test.

Case 2 is why this skill exists. A pure red-flag checklist deletes this person.

## Six questions before you pay

1. What share of their income comes from doing the work, and what share from teaching it?
2. Can you name one student like you, with a result, found outside their channels?
3. When did the founder last REPLY to someone (not broadcast)?
4. Who mentioned them without being paid to, and how long ago was the first time?
5. What have they publicly said they were wrong about?
6. What result did their free material already produce for you?

## When the evidence is thin

Most real checks end at "not enough signal," not at a clean buy or skip. Do not invent confidence. That is the failure this skill is trying to catch. Offer the cheap next step instead:

- Join the free tier if there is one. Run the overclaiming probe and the calibration probe for a week or two before any paid plan.
- Pay for one month of the cheapest plan, and measure founder-reply latency yourself, instead of trusting "the founder is very active."

## What this skill does not do

- It does not decide whether you can afford the purchase. Pair it with your own spending rule. This skill only answers "if you spend, on whom."
- It does not contact, register, or buy on your behalf.
- It is not legal or financial due diligence.
- It does not state that a named person is a fraud as a fact. It states observed signals and how far they were verified. If a criterion has no evidence, write "not verified." Do not convert the gap into a good signal or a bad one.
- It will not catch every case. It is an 80/20 net, not a full investigation.

## Before you deliver the check

- Did you search the name outside their own channels?
- Did you check founder-reply recency, not only founder-post recency?
- Did you run at least one active probe if you had a way to interact?
- Did you list what you could NOT verify, instead of treating the gap as a positive?
- Did you open with the table, then the evidence?
- Did you open the original source for every specific number, including numbers that help your current verdict?
- Did you apply the same skepticism to evidence for the verdict as to evidence against it?
